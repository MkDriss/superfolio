Octave is a music quiz platform designed for seniors living with Alzheimer's disease, built in a team of four as a school project. Music can bring back memories and emotions in just a few notes, so we built fully customizable quizzes around songs, a configuration space where day care staff adapt the game to each participant, and a real-time multiplayer mode. Shaped from start to finish by the feedback of a real day care center, Octave won both the Jury Prize and the Audience Prize at the 2025 Fullstack Contest.

The brief

During our first year of engineering school at Polytech Nice Sophia, we had to build a web application with Angular, following a user-centered design approach. Teams of four had to choose between two very different audiences.

The first option was a set of games for children with learning disorders such as dyslexia, dyscalculia or dyspraxia, guided by occupational therapists working with them. The second was a platform for seniors living with Alzheimer's, Parkinson's or other age-related conditions, attending a day care center in partnership with the school, and guided by its staff.

Whatever the choice, the platform needed a configuration space for administrators: a place to collect statistics on each player, and to adjust the game to a specific person to help them along.

Choosing our audience

We chose the seniors. The subject echoed personal experiences for some of us, and the challenge was all the greater: the platform would be used by people who rarely, if ever, use a computer.

One difference shaped the whole project. A child can make progress with their learning disorder; Alzheimer's, on the other hand, cannot be reversed, and the best one can hope for is to slow its progression. So our goal was never to make participants « win » or improve, but to stimulate them, and above all to give them a good time.

Finding the right idea

Our first instinct was classic memory games, like a memory card game or putting objects back in place in a room. We wanted something more original, and found it in music therapy: sometimes, a few notes are enough to bring back a memory or an emotion. That is how the idea of customizable music quizzes was born.

We also knew how quiet day care centers can be, and how much participants can lack stimulation, despite all the staff's efforts. So we imagined a multiplayer mode, to bring a touch of friendly competition. As we would discover later, it turned into something else entirely.

Understanding before coding

Before writing a single line of code, we took the time to understand the needs. We presented our ideas and questions to the day care staff during dedicated meetings, and formalized the expected behavior with use case and sequence diagrams.

At the same time, we were introduced to component-based design with Matrio, a simplified Python framework built by a Polytech student, which takes Angular's component concepts and strips them down to the essentials. We used it to build our first mockups, then showed them to the staff.

Their feedback made the application better from the start: this is how customizable hints, revealed one by one during the game, came to life.

Playing without frustration

People living with Alzheimer's can easily lose track. The challenge was to find the right balance: enough stimulation to keep them in the game, without overloading them with information. And above all, a participant should never feel uncomfortable or frustrated while playing.

So every help can be adjusted to each participant by the staff:

  • Hints appear one by one, as the question goes on.
  • Wrong answers disappear when a participant takes too long, after a delay set in advance.
  • A message offers to move on to the next question after a while, rather than leaving someone stuck.

The game reacts kindly, too. When a participant picks a wrong answer, it simply disappears, with no penalty, and when they find the right one, a warm message in encouraging colors invites them to keep going.

Building it step by step

The project was also about learning web development and Angular, a framework widely used in the industry. We built Octave one feature at a time, starting with a simple, working version of the game.

The single-player mode came first. Then an administration page to create and configure participant profiles, followed by a quiz editor, so that quizzes and participants were no longer hard-coded test data. Next came the statistics, collected during every game. Finally, the multiplayer mode, which wasn't required, but which we wanted badly enough to take on the extra work.

Once the frontend was complete, our teacher provided a minimal JavaScript backend to plug in a database, and we containerized the whole project with Docker.

Playing together

This was our first time working with WebSockets. Before coding, we listed every action each player could take, at every moment of a game, to make sure no situation was left unhandled.

A game led by the staff

A staff member hosts the game: they create a lobby with a unique code. At first, participants joined with that code; after discussing it with the staff, we added a simpler way: the host sees which participants are connected, and adds them with a single click. The participant's screen then lets them know they'll soon join a game. While they wait, a short tutorial explains how to play.

One screen to lead, one screen to answer

The host's screen shows the question, the hints, and plays the music. We chose a single sound source on purpose: a dozen devices playing at once would have created a din and disturbed participants. Their screen shows only the answers, nothing more.

Answers that spark conversation

The host moves through the questions at their own pace, and sees the share of each answer update live. When everyone agrees, or when opinions are split, it becomes a natural starting point for a discussion.

Feedback for everyone

Once a participant answers, they wait for the others before the answer is revealed: a rewarding message if they found it, an encouraging one if not. At the end of the game, everyone is congratulated. The host can then go back over the game: questions, answer shares, hints, replay a song people enjoyed, or show the leaderboard.

Following each participant

This is where the configuration space takes on its full meaning: statistics help the staff adapt the game to each person.

For each quiz, the staff can see how many times it was played, the average time and number of hints per question, and how answers were distributed for each question.

For each participant, they can see how many quizzes were played over a year, and how their results evolve month by month: share of correct answers, average time per question, hints used. These trends help spot a sudden decline. Every past game, solo or in a group, can be opened in detail to better understand a score.

Meeting the participants

Near the end of the project, we had the chance to welcome participants from the day care center at Polytech, to try Octave for themselves. It was an opportunity to meet the people we had been designing for, listen to them, and see how they actually used the platform. It was also a deeply moving moment.

And they surprised us. The multiplayer mode, designed to bring some competition, turned into something else: participants helped each other, discussed the answers, and talked together. In places like day care centers, creating these bonds matters enormously, and it became, for us, the project's most meaningful achievement.

The Fullstack Contest

It was our first time on that stage, and our project was selected among the best of the class.

Each year, the best projects of the class are selected to compete in the Fullstack Contest, and taking part earns each team member a title of excellence awarded by the school. The format is inspired by « Ma thèse en 180 secondes »: each team pitches its project on stage, in front of a lecture hall filled with first-year students and a jury of company representatives. The pitch is followed by a speed-dating session, where each team meets the jury members to go deeper into the project and answer their questions. The jury then establishes a ranking to award the Jury Prize, while the audience votes for the Audience Prize.

Octave won both!🥇🥇🎉

A year later, we did it again with Snell.
