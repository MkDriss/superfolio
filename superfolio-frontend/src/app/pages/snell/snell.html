Snell is a multiplayer web version of Khet, the laser chess game, built in a team of two as a school project. The catch: no frameworks, only vanilla JavaScript. So we built our own lightweight HTTP framework, and designed a microservice architecture able to absorb a new feature every week.

Beyond the brief, we added an AI opponent with several difficulty levels, an interactive tutorial, puzzles, a shop, an Android app, real-time monitoring and encrypted backups, and I self-hosted the whole platform on a Raspberry Pi. It won both the Jury Prize and the Audience Prize at the 2026 Fullstack Contest.

The context

Snell started as a school assignment with a simple premise: build an online version of Khet, a board game where players use mirrors to guide a laser toward the opposing king. Our teacher reworked the original rules into his own version, and set a pace that would shape the entire project: every week, a new feature to deliver, until the final version.

We didn't start from a blank page, but close to it. The starter project gave us a simple HTTP server able to serve files, and the Socket.IO code for real-time communication. Everything else had to be built by hand: frameworks were forbidden, and only a short list of libraries was allowed, for tasks where rebuilding them would teach little, such as WebSockets or password hashing. The goal was clear: understand how the features of a full-stack application work natively, before ever relying on abstractions.

Looking back, these two constraints ended up driving almost every decision we made. The rest of this page tells how.

The Game

The rules are a reworked version of Khet, played on a 10×10 grid. Each player controls five kinds of pieces, which we renamed for our version:

The Shooter, an indestructible piece that fires the laser at the end of every turn.

The Protector, shielded on one side only.

The Triangles, whose single mirror redirects the beam.

The Full Mirror, which reflects on both sides and can never be destroyed.

The King, vulnerable from every angle. Lose your King, and you lose the game.

On each turn, a player performs exactly one action: rotate a piece, move it, place a Triangle from their reserve, or swap their Full Mirror with their Shooter or their King, each swap having its own cooldown. Then the laser fires, bounces off the mirrors, and destroys every unprotected piece in its path. A destroyed Triangle isn't gone for good: it joins the opponent's reserve, ready to be used against its former owner. And every game starts differently, since the initial positions are randomized and mirrored around the center of the board.

For the look and feel, we picked a theme with a wink: Snell is a playful parody of chess.com, from its colors to its crossed-out logo, all the way to our very own coach, Bae.

Building everything by hand

Without a framework, every HTTP request had to be handled from scratch: parsing the URL, reading the body, routing it to the right function, sending back a response. After writing the same logic a few times, we did what any framework does under the hood: we extracted it into our own lightweight, Express-like framework, with routing and middlewares. Writing it ourselves meant understanding exactly what happens between a request arriving and a response leaving, which is precisely what libraries are designed to hide.

An architecture built to grow

With a new feature every week, we needed an architecture where adding something wouldn't mean breaking everything else. Splitting the platform into microservices felt like the natural answer: each domain lives in its own service (game, users, chat, shop, achievements, mail...), runs in its own Docker container, and can evolve independently. A single gateway sits in front of them: it is the only entry point reachable from the outside, and it checks authentication before forwarding each request to the right service.

This choice paid off week after week. When a new feature came in, it often meant adding a new service and plugging it into the gateway, without touching the rest.

Learning to play

The brief asked for a tutorial, or a text explaining the rules. A wall of text felt like the fastest way to lose new players, so we built something interactive instead: Coach Bae walks you through the rules step by step, while the board highlights the cells you need to interact with.

Once the basics are in place, puzzles take over: tricky positions to solve, with hints when you're stuck, to practice the kind of situations that decide real games.

Playing

Local and against the AI. Two players can face each other on the same screen, or take on one of our AI opponents, each with its own level and personality (more on them below). Against the AI, players also choose their color: white, black or random, white always moving first, as in chess.

Online, in real time. Multiplayer games are synchronized in real time through Socket.IO, and every move is validated by the server: as our teacher put it, never trust your users. Rather than pairing players at random, our matchmaking compares their Elo ratings to find an opponent of similar level. If no match turns up after a while, it gradually widens the acceptable rating gap, so nobody waits forever. Ratings are updated after every online game, and the gap between the two players matters: the bigger it is, the more the underdog stands to win, and the favorite to lose.

Inside the AI

Our first AI played at random: enough to check that the engine knew every legal move, not enough to make a real opponent. Each improvement that followed answers a specific problem.

I. Thinking ahead

To play well, the AI has to imagine its move, then the opponent's best reply, then its own answer, and so on. That is exactly what minimax does (here in its negamax form). But the possibilities multiply at every step, so the AI drops any line as soon as it is proven worse than one it has already found (alpha-beta pruning).

II. Too many moves

In a typical starting position, a player has 343 possible actions, and 324 of them are Triangle placements that mostly change nothing. Since the laser is fully predictable, a move can only matter if it touches the beam's path or the shooter itself. So the AI looks at those moves first, along with the ones that already worked elsewhere in its search (killer moves, history heuristic), and gives the harmless-looking ones only a quick glance, unless they turn out better than expected (principal variation search, late move reductions, futility pruning).

III. Limited time

The AI never knows in advance how deep it will manage to search. So it starts one move ahead, then goes deeper and deeper until its time runs out, always keeping the best move found so far (iterative deepening). Along the way, it remembers the positions it has already analyzed, since the same one can be reached in different orders, recognizing each by a compact fingerprint (transposition table, Zobrist hashing).

IV. Stopping at the wrong moment

A position can look excellent right after your own shot, and terrible right after your opponent's. So before judging a position, the AI plays out any exchange of shots still pending (quiescence search).

V. Judging a position

In laser chess, only the beam kills: chess intuitions like controlling the center don't apply. So the AI looks at what each laser would destroy if it fired now, and at how exposed each King is to a redirected beam.

VI. Speed

All of this means weighing hundreds of thousands of positions every turn. To keep up, each piece fits in a single byte, the whole board in 100 bytes, and the search creates no new objects along the way.

VII. A ladder of opponents

All the AIs share the same engine; only their profile changes: thinking time, temperament, and how often they make mistakes. Making a weak AI short-sighted would make it oddly sharp at times, so instead it occasionally picks a lesser move, though never one that loses its King on the spot, just like a beginner. The ladder goes from Patrick La Chine (500 Elo), who plays randomly, to Bot Bizot (2500 Elo), who is a formidable opponent.

These techniques come from chess engines: the references at the end of this page explain them in more depth.

Reviewing a game

Beyond the brief, any past game, online or against the AI, can be replayed move by move. An evaluation function analyzes the board at each step to estimate which player has the advantage, so you can spot the exact move where a game turned.

Playing together

Players create an account, add friends (a request on one side, an approval on the other) and challenge them to a duel. The brief asked for one kind of chat out of three: a global chat, a private chat between friends, or an in-game chat. We built all three.

Reasons to come back

The brief asked for achievements and a leaderboard, with an Elo ranking for every profile. We built a whole progression system around them. Completing an achievement now rewards players with SnellCoins, our in-game currency, which they can spend in a shop inspired by Fortnite's: profile pictures, emote packs for the chat, and themes that change the look of the pieces. Everything they buy lands in their inventory. SnellCoins can also be bought through a Stripe payment link, running in sandbox mode for the exercise.

Accounts and security

Some areas, such as online games and the AI, require being signed in: sessions rely on JWT tokens, checked by the gateway, and passwords are hashed before being stored. Forgotten passwords can be reset by email. And for the fun of it, we hid an alternative way in: the honesty test. First, declare whether you're honest. Then, drag and drop a set of statements into the « Liar » and « Honest » boxes. Get the combination right after claiming to be honest, and you've proven you can be trusted... with resetting anyone's password. Needless to say, this one is a joke, not a security feature.

On Android

The project also had to ship as a mobile app. We wrapped the platform with Capacitor, redesigned the interface for touch screens, and integrated a native feature of the device: local notifications, triggered when you receive a friend request or a challenge request.

Running it for real

Shipping features was one thing; keeping the platform healthy was another. We added a monitoring page fed by the Docker socket and dedicated API endpoints: it shows the number of games in progress, the state of each container, and so of each service, and the CPU and memory usage of the host machine.

The database is backed up automatically every night at 2 AM, when the fewest players are online. Three scripts handle the whole cycle: one creates the backup and encrypts it with AES-256, one checks its integrity by simulating a full restoration, and one restores the database when needed. Backups are kept on a rolling 14-day window, so they never saturate the storage. The next step would have been to store them on a separate server, so that losing the machine wouldn't mean losing the backups too.

From the cloud to my desk

The course planned to deploy every project on an AWS instance. I chose to host Snell myself, on my own Raspberry Pi: the services run with Docker Compose behind an nginx reverse proxy, and all traffic comes in through a Cloudflare Tunnel, so no port is ever opened on my home network.

The Fullstack Contest

Each year, the best projects of the class are selected to compete in the Fullstack Contest, and taking part earns each team member a title of excellence awarded by the school. The format is inspired by « Ma thèse en 180 secondes »: each team pitches its project on stage, in front of a lecture hall filled with first-year students and a jury of company representatives. The pitch is followed by a speed-dating session, where each team meets the jury members to go deeper into the project and answer their questions. The jury then establishes a ranking to award the Jury Prize, while the audience votes for the Audience Prize.

This year, Snell won both!🥇🥇🎉
